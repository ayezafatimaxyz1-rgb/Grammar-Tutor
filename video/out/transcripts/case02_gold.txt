Grammar Detective, MATERIAL
Narration: George (British English, male)

[0:00.35] A ring. A coin. A necklace. Three golden things. Now watch: melt them down. How many golds do you have now?

[0:08.58] Three? One? The question doesn’t even work. And that’s the whole lesson, with no memorising.

[0:15.22] Case two: materials. Why do we say ‘made of gold’, and never ‘made of golds’?

[0:21.25] Proof one: the cut test. Cut a ring in half. Is it still a ring? No. It’s a broken ring.

[0:28.28] Now cut a lump of gold in half. Still gold? Yes. Cut it again. Still gold. However small, it’s still gold.

[0:37.38] Proof two. So how do we give a number? Count the objects: three rings. Or measure the material: twenty grams of gold. The number never lands on gold.

[0:48.65] Proof three, the twist. Bricks can be counted: this wall has forty bricks. But brick is also a material, like gold.

[0:57.25] So both are right. ‘Made of bricks’ means the blocks. ‘Made of brick’ means the material. The meaning decides the grammar.

[1:06.45] Your turn: wood. Cut a plank in half. Still wood? Yes. So: ‘This table is made of wood.’

[1:13.55] So don’t memorise. Cut it in half. If every piece is still the same stuff, it’s a material. Measure it, don’t count it.
