### Chapter 2: The Importance of Vocabulary (pages 11 to 20)

**Main idea.** A précis keeps the passage's meaning, tone and nuance in fewer words, and the right word is what makes that possible. A small vocabulary loses the fine details.

**2.1 Why vocabulary matters**
- **Choosing the exact word:** *solitude, isolation, loneliness* and *seclusion* are different. *Isolation* is unwanted and negative; *solitude* is chosen and can be peaceful.
- **Accuracy:** one exact word can carry a long phrase (*imminent* for "rapidly approaching").
- **Conciseness:** abstract nouns carry a whole clause:
  - *technological ingenuity* for "an innovative approach to technology"
  - *financial prosperity* for "doing exceptionally well financially"

**2.2 The building blocks: word types**
- **Nouns:** one noun for a phrase ("a person who teaches" is an *educator*).
- **Verbs:** one direct verb for a noun phrase ("is conducting an examination of" is *is reviewing*).
- **Adjectives:** keep only the essential ones.
- **Adverbs:** *very, really, extremely* can usually go.
- **Etymology** (where a word comes from) shows its deeper meaning: *hospitality* (Latin *hospes*, host or guest), *democracy* (Greek *demos*, people, and *kratos*, rule).

**2.3 Learning words**
- Ways to learn: flashcards, wide reading, spaced repetition, guessing from context before looking a word up, a word of the day.
- **Denotation** is the literal meaning (*home* is where someone lives). **Connotation** is the feeling a word carries (*home* means warmth and safety).
- *Frugal* (positive) and *stingy* (negative) both mean careful with money.

**2.4 Context changes meaning**
- *sanction*: a penalty, or an approval
- *cultivate*: crops, or friendships
- *novel*: a book of fiction, or new
- *reserve*: backup troops, or a protected area
- in medicine, *malignant* is not just *harmful*

**2.5 The chapter's word list**
- *concise*: succinct, terse, laconic
- *elucidate*: clarify, expound, illuminate
- *emphasize*: stress, highlight, underscore
- *negate*: nullify, invalidate, cancel out
- *contrast*: differentiate, distinguish, juxtapose

**2.6 Case studies**
- Plain words for legal and medical jargon.
- In Pakistan, hard words learned by heart make a précis wordy and unclear: a word is only useful when it can be used correctly.

**2.7 Précis vocabulary:** *henceforth, notwithstanding, thus, namely, in essence, in sum*.

**2.8 Common mistakes, and the remedies**
- Mistakes:
  - a word used wrongly (*nefarious* for negative)
  - needlessly complex words (*utilize, ameliorate*)
  - redundancy ("in the final analysis" is just *finally*)
  - mixed idioms
  - ignoring connotation (a peaceful protest called *riotous*)
- Remedies: use several dictionaries, practise words in context, ask a reviewer, read your writing aloud.

**The chapter's exercises**
1. Shorten a long sentence about an investigation and its confounding variables.
2. Use *henceforth*, *thus* and *namely* in sentences.

### Chapter 3: The Art of Choosing Synonyms (pages 22 to 31)

**Main idea.** Most "synonyms" are near-synonyms: the same basic meaning, but a different tone, formality, strength, connotation or technical sense. A précis must keep all of these.

**3.1 What a synonym can do**
- Shorten (*disseminate* becomes *spread*), and avoid repeating a word.
- But it can also change:
  - the tone (*said* / *exclaimed*)
  - the formality (*use* / *utilize*)
  - the strength (*happy* / *ecstatic*)

**3.2 Context**
- **Keep the essence:** *arduous* is not just *long*.
- **Be culturally sensitive:** *exotic* becomes *distinctive*.
- **Be technically accurate:** a *disease* is not a *syndrome*.
- **Poor choices:**
  - alter the meaning: *ambiguous* (two readings) is not *vague* (unclear)
  - confuse: *gamble* for *risk*
  - change the emotion: *liking* for *adoration*

**3.3 Near-synonyms differ in**
- **connotation:** *thrifty* / *stingy*
- **context:** *ask* / *inquire*
- **specificity:** *happy* / *elated*

The risks: *confident* is not *arrogant*; *like* is not *love*; *hypothesis* is not *theory*.

**3.4 Finding the right synonym**
- Read the passage deeply first: is *meticulous* thorough, or nitpicky?
- Use a thesaurus with caution: *cryptic* means deliberately hidden.
- Check the word in real sentences.
- Avoid extreme words (*crucial* for *important*).
- Use several sources, and ask a reviewer.

**3.5 Exercise formats**
- Replace a word.
- Condense a sentence.
- Change the tone: "The results were good" becomes *satisfactory* (formal) or *excellent* (positive).
- Each time, check the context, tone, nuance and sources.

**3.6 Case studies (good and bad choices)**
- *mental health* for *psychological well-being* (good); *feeling good* for *emotional stability* (bad)
- *revenue growth* for *financial augmentation* (good); *money-making* for *profitable* (too informal)
- *instructional strategy* for *educational methodology* (good); *schooling stuff* for *educational materials* (bad)

**3.7 Tone and style**
- Formality: *commence* / *begin*
- Emotional weight: *infuriated* / *angry*
- Technical vs general words
- Match the passage's tone, and keep it the same throughout.

**3.8 Specialized fields**
- Law: *tort* is not *wrong*
- Medicine: *myocardial infarction* / *heart attack*
- Journalism: *insurgent* / *rebel*
- Engineering: *tensile strength* is not *durability*

### Where the book is wrong (found while reading it)
- **Its condensing example gets longer:** it goes from 10 to 11 words and swaps *hypothesis* for *theory*, the very error 3.3 warns against.
- **Utilize is not formal:** it is inflated, and Chapter 2 says so itself.
- **Exaggerating:** "an extremely intelligent individual" is not *a genius*; *a brilliant person* keeps the strength.
- **Weakening:** "overwhelming dread" is not *apprehensive*; *dreaded* keeps the strength.
- **Wrong English:** "The drug effectively improved the disease" is incorrect, because a drug *treats* a disease.
- **"The concerned party"** can read as "the worried party"; standard English is *the party concerned*.

## Part 2. What the learner said she wants

### For the app as a whole
- A **separate app, only for précis**.
- To take her "**from zero to hero**" using the methods of this book, which she finds excellent, and to become an expert in précis: "not just AI slop, but a proper thing".
- She is sending the rest of the book's chapters, and other material, for this.

### Vocabulary section 1: one short word for a long phrase
> "There should be a vocabulary section of one type that tells, for a long sentence, we have this short word."

A section where a long phrase or sentence is replaced by its one short word.

### Vocabulary section 2: synonyms and antonyms, by meaning
> "One section like, if I write *happy*, all of its synonyms and antonyms with its tone, formality level, style, context, which are real synonyms for one context. For example, we have *alone*: *alone* by choice, synonyms in that sense, and in other."

A section where she types a word (like *happy*) and gets:
- all of its **synonyms** and **antonyms**
- for each one: its **tone**, its **formality level**, its **style** and its **context**
- only the **real synonyms for one context (one meaning)**, with each meaning of the word shown separately.

Her example is *alone*:
- *alone* by choice, with the synonyms for that sense
- *alone* in its other senses, each with its own synonyms
